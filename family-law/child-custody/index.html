Child Custody & Visitation: Guardianship, Interim Arrangements and Contact

“Where will the children live, and how will they see the other parent?” When parents separate, the law’s first concern is the child’s welfare, not the parents’ rights. This page explains custody, guardianship and visitation, and how arrangements work in practice.

Child Custody & Visitation: Guardianship, Interim Arrangements and Contact

Custody, guardianship and visitation: what each means

  • Custody is about where the child lives day to day and who looks after them. One parent may have custody while the other has visitation, or the parents may share it.
  • Guardianship is the legal authority to take decisions for the child and the child’s property — schooling, medical treatment, travel and so on. A parent who does not have custody usually remains a guardian.
  • Visitation, or access, is the time the child spends with the parent who does not have custody, and the contact they keep, including by telephone and video call.

The Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 applies to all communities. For Hindus, the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 also applies, and in matrimonial cases the court can make custody orders under section 26 of the Hindu Marriage Act (section 38 of the Special Marriage Act).

How courts consider the child’s welfare

Section 13 of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act makes the welfare of the child the paramount consideration, and the Guardians and Wards Act likewise directs the court to be guided by the child’s welfare. In practice the court looks at:

  • the child’s age, health and emotional needs
  • who has been the main carer, and the stability of the child’s present home and school
  • each parent’s capacity, availability and conduct towards the child
  • the child’s own preference, where the child is old enough to form an intelligent preference; judges often speak to the child in private
  • keeping brothers and sisters together

The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act provides that the custody of a child under five years ordinarily remains with the mother. That is a starting point, not a rule that overrides welfare. Neither parent has an automatic right to custody of an older child; nor does a parent’s higher income decide the question.

Interim arrangements while the case is pending

Custody cases take time, and the child cannot wait. The court can make interim orders on where the child will live and how the other parent will have contact until the case is decided. Interim arrangements often shape the final one, because courts are reluctant to disturb a settled routine. It is therefore worth asking for interim contact early rather than waiting.

Practical examples of visitation arrangements

Visitation orders and agreements work best when they are specific. Some illustrations of the kind of terms parents and courts use:

Examples of specific contact terms
SituationExample of a specific arrangement
WeekendsThe child stays with the other parent from Saturday 10 a.m. to Sunday 6 p.m. on alternate weekends
School holidaysThe summer vacation is divided in two halves, alternating each year; Diwali or Eid alternates between parents
HandoversHandover at the gate of the child’s school or another agreed neutral place, so that the parents do not need to meet inside each other’s homes
Video callsFixed calls, for example at 7 p.m. on Wednesdays and Sundays for twenty minutes, without the other parent interrupting
Birthdays and school eventsBoth parents may attend school functions; the child spends part of the birthday with each parent
TravelNeither parent takes the child outside India without the other’s written consent or the court’s permission; passports are kept as agreed

These are illustrations, not standard terms. The right arrangement depends on the child’s age, school timings and the distance between homes.

Document checklist

  • The child’s birth certificate
  • School records: admission, fee receipts, report cards, attendance
  • Medical records and the name of the child’s doctor
  • Evidence of who has cared for the child day to day
  • Details of each parent’s home, work hours and support (for example grandparents)
  • Any earlier custody or contact order, and a record of how it has been followed
