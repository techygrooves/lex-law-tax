Dowry Harassment & Matrimonial Cruelty: Complaints, FIRs and Responding

“The police have registered a cruelty case — what do the sections mean and what happens next?” Matrimonial cruelty and dowry demands are criminal offences. This page explains complaints and FIRs, police notices, responding to allegations, and why some papers cite the Indian Penal Code and others the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita.

Dowry Harassment & Matrimonial Cruelty: Complaints, FIRs and Responding

Why case papers use old or new section numbers

On 1 July 2024 the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS) replaced the Indian Penal Code, 1860, and the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS) replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973. Offences committed before that date are charged under the IPC; offences committed on or after it, under the BNS. Proceedings that were already pending continue under the old procedure. So a case about events in 2023 may cite section 498A IPC, and one about events in 2025 section 85 BNS, although the conduct is the same.

Main provisions, old and new
SubjectBefore 1 July 2024From 1 July 2024
Cruelty by husband or relativesSection 498A IPCSections 85 (offence) and 86 (definition of cruelty) BNS
Dowry deathSection 304B IPCSection 80 BNS
Criminal breach of trust (for example, keeping stridhan)Section 406 IPCSection 316 BNS
Notice to appear before the policeSection 41A CrPCSection 35(3) BNSS
High Court’s power to quash proceedingsSection 482 CrPCSection 528 BNSS

The Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 was not replaced and continues to apply. It makes giving, taking and demanding dowry offences. The cruelty provision carries imprisonment of up to three years and a fine; dowry death carries a minimum of seven years. Statutory texts are on India Code.

Making a complaint

  • A complaint of cruelty or dowry harassment can be made at the police station with jurisdiction, in writing. If the police do not act, the law provides further routes: a complaint to the Superintendent of Police, or an application to the Magistrate.
  • Describe each incident with its date, place, who was involved and what was said or done. General statements against every family member without specific facts are treated with caution by courts.
  • Keep copies of everything you submit, and of the FIR or receipt you are given.

The cooling period and Family Welfare Committees in Uttar Pradesh

In Uttar Pradesh, the Allahabad High Court laid down guidelines in 2022 (Mukesh Bansal v. State of U.P.) for cases under the cruelty provision where the other offences alleged carry less than ten years’ imprisonment and there is no injury. Under those guidelines no arrest or coercive action is to be taken for two months after the FIR or complaint, and the matter is referred to the Family Welfare Committee of the district during that period. The Supreme Court endorsed these guidelines in July 2025 (Shivangi Bansal v. Sahib Bansal) and directed that they be implemented.

The cooling period is not a decision on the merits, and it does not stop a complaint being registered. It gives the parties and the Committee time before the police take coercive steps.

If you have received a police notice or are named in an FIR

  • Read the notice for the date, time and place you are asked to appear. A notice under section 35(3) BNSS (formerly 41A CrPC) requires you to appear; complying with it is important.
  • Get a copy of the FIR. Check which sections are invoked and whether the events alleged are before or after 1 July 2024.
  • Do not contact the complainant to pressure or argue; it can be treated as intimidation.
  • Collect documents that answer the allegations: messages, bank records, travel records showing where you were, and names of witnesses.
  • Depending on the facts, anticipatory bail, a challenge in the High Court, or settlement through mediation may be options. A settlement does not end a cruelty case by itself, because the offence cannot be compounded by the parties; the usual route after settlement is an application to the High Court to quash the proceedings.

Document checklist

  • The complaint, FIR and any notice received
  • Proof of marriage and the date of the events
  • Messages, emails, call records and recordings
  • Medical records, if any
  • Lists of dowry items or demands, with any supporting proof
  • Bank statements showing payments made or demanded
  • Names and contact details of witnesses
