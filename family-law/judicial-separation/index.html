Judicial Separation: Living Apart, a Separation Order and Divorce Compared

“We are living apart. Are we legally separated?” Not in the eyes of the law. Living apart is a fact; judicial separation is a court order; divorce ends the marriage. This page explains what each means and what happens to the marriage in each case.

Judicial Separation: Living Apart, a Separation Order and Divorce Compared

Living apart, judicial separation and divorce compared

People often describe themselves as “separated” when they have simply stopped living together. That has legal consequences — it is, for example, the starting point for a mutual consent divorce — but it is not a legal status. The table sets out the three situations.

What each situation means
Living apartJudicial separationDivorce
What it isA fact: the spouses no longer live as husband and wifeA court decree that the spouses are no longer obliged to live togetherA court decree dissolving the marriage
Does the marriage continue?YesYesNo
Can either spouse remarry?NoNoYes, once the decree is final
Court involved?NoYes, on a petition and a proved groundYes
Can it lead to divorce?One year of separation is a condition for mutual consent divorceIf the spouses do not resume living together for one year after the decree, either may seek divorce—

What a decree of judicial separation does

Under section 10 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 (and section 23 of the Special Marriage Act, 1954), either spouse may ask the court for judicial separation on any of the grounds on which a divorce could be sought. Once the decree is passed, the petitioner is no longer obliged to live with the other spouse.

The marriage itself continues. Neither spouse can marry anyone else, and the rights that depend on the marriage — including maintenance and inheritance — continue unless and until a divorce decree is passed. The court can cancel the decree if the spouses reconcile and ask it to.

Judicial separation suits people who want the legal recognition and protection of being apart but are not ready to end the marriage — for religious, family or personal reasons, or because they want time before a final decision.

When separation can become divorce

If the spouses have not resumed living together for one year or more after a decree of judicial separation, either of them — including the spouse against whom the decree was passed — may petition for divorce on that ground alone. The question in that later petition is not who was at fault originally but whether cohabitation was resumed.

Simply living apart without any decree does not, by itself, give a ground for contested divorce, although desertion for two years may, if its legal requirements are met. Where both spouses agree, one year of living apart is enough for a mutual consent petition.

Document checklist

  • Proof of marriage
  • A dated account of the events relied on as the ground (for example cruelty or desertion)
  • Evidence of when and why the spouses began living apart: messages, letters, a change of address, police or medical records
  • Proof of each spouse’s current address
  • Income and expense records if maintenance is to be claimed
