Marriage Annulment & Nullity: When a Marriage Is Void or Voidable

“Our marriage should never have happened. Is that different from divorce?” It can be. A divorce ends a valid marriage; annulment declares that a valid marriage never existed, or sets aside one that had a defect from the start. This page explains the difference and the time limits that apply.

Marriage Annulment & Nullity: When a Marriage Is Void or Voidable

Annulment and divorce are different questions

A divorce looks at what happened after the wedding — cruelty, desertion, and so on. A petition for nullity (often called annulment) looks at the position at the time of the wedding: was there a legal impediment, or a defect in consent, that meant the marriage was not valid?

The answer depends on which law governs the marriage. A defect that makes a marriage void under one Act may not have the same effect under another. That is why the first question is always whether the marriage was solemnised under the Hindu Marriage Act, the Special Marriage Act or another personal law.

Void marriages

A void marriage is treated as never having existed. Under section 11 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, a marriage is void if, at the time of the marriage, either party had a living spouse, or the parties were within the prohibited degrees of relationship or were sapindas of each other (close relatives defined by the Act), unless a custom governing both of them permits the marriage. Either party may ask the court for a decree declaring the marriage void.

Under the Special Marriage Act, 1954 (section 24), a marriage is void if any of the conditions for marriage under the Act were not met — for example if either party had a living spouse, was under age, or was within the prohibited degrees — or if the respondent was impotent at the time of the marriage and at the time of the petition.

Voidable marriages

A voidable marriage is valid until a court sets it aside on a petition by the affected spouse. Under section 12 of the Hindu Marriage Act, the grounds include:

  • the marriage has not been consummated because of the respondent’s impotence
  • the respondent, at the time of the marriage, was incapable of giving valid consent because of unsoundness of mind, or suffered from a mental disorder making them unfit for marriage
  • the petitioner’s consent, or that of a guardian where it was required, was obtained by force or by fraud about the nature of the ceremony or a material fact or circumstance concerning the respondent
  • the respondent was, at the time of the marriage, pregnant by someone other than the petitioner

There are strict conditions. A petition on the ground of force or fraud must be presented within one year after the force ceased or the fraud was discovered, and is not allowed if the petitioner lived with the other spouse as husband or wife with full consent after that. A petition on the ground of pregnancy has its own conditions as to the petitioner’s knowledge and the time of filing. The Special Marriage Act (section 25) has comparable provisions.

What happens to children of a void or voidable marriage

The law protects children of such marriages. Under section 16 of the Hindu Marriage Act, a child of a void or voidable marriage is legitimate, whether born before or after the decree. Such a child has rights in the property of the parents. The court can also make orders about custody and maintenance in the nullity proceedings.

Document checklist

  • Proof of the marriage and its date
  • Evidence of the defect relied on: proof of an earlier marriage, of age, of the relationship between the parties, or medical evidence
  • For force or fraud: when the force ceased or the fraud was discovered, and how; messages and documents that show it
  • Birth certificates of any children

Questions

Questions People Ask

Under the Hindu Marriage Act and the Special Marriage Act, a marriage with a person who had a living spouse is void. You can ask the court to declare it void. Bigamy is also an offence, and maintenance and other rights may still be available depending on the facts.

For a void marriage there is no fixed period within which the declaration must be sought. For a voidable marriage on the ground of force or fraud, the petition must be presented within one year of the force ceasing or the fraud being discovered.

Muslim law distinguishes between a marriage that is void (batil) and one that is irregular (fasid), and the consequences differ. The question is answered under Muslim personal law, not the Hindu Marriage Act, unless the marriage was solemnised under the Special Marriage Act.
