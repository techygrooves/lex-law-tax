Family Court Mediation: What Happens, How to Prepare and Possible Outcomes

“The court has sent us to mediation. What happens there?” Mediation is a confidential meeting in which a trained mediator helps the spouses work out their own agreement. Nobody can be forced to agree. This page explains what happens, how to prepare, and what follows each possible outcome.

Family Court Mediation: What Happens, How to Prepare and Possible Outcomes

Why family cases go to mediation

Section 9 of the Family Courts Act, 1984 requires the Family Court to make efforts to persuade the parties to a settlement, and the Code of Civil Procedure provides for referral of disputes to mediation. Family courts commonly refer matrimonial disputes — divorce, maintenance, custody, domestic violence and related criminal matters — to the mediation centre attached to the court, usually at an early hearing. The Mediation Act, 2023 now also provides a statutory framework for mediation, and parts of it have been brought into force.

What happens in a session

  • The mediator, usually an advocate or a retired judicial officer trained in mediation, explains the process and its confidentiality.
  • Each spouse explains their position. The mediator often meets each separately as well as together.
  • The mediator helps identify what each person actually needs — for example a secure income, time with the children, the return of belongings — and explores options. The mediator does not decide anything.
  • Several sessions may be needed. Lawyers may attend or be consulted, depending on the centre’s practice.
  • What is said in mediation is confidential and cannot be used as evidence in court if the mediation fails.

Which issues can be discussed

  • Whether the marriage will continue, and if not, whether the spouses will seek a mutual consent divorce
  • Permanent alimony and maintenance, including amounts and payment dates
  • Custody of the children, visitation, holidays, schooling and expenses
  • Return of stridhan, jewellery and belongings
  • Jointly owned property, loans and accounts
  • What happens to all the other cases between the parties

How to prepare

  • List what matters most to you, and what you could accept, before the first session.
  • Bring a realistic monthly budget and income documents if money is in issue.
  • Bring the stridhan inventory and supporting photographs if belongings are in issue.
  • For children, think through a practical weekly and holiday schedule.
  • Bring a list of all pending cases with their numbers, courts and next dates.

Full, partial or no agreement

What follows each outcome
OutcomeWhat happens next
Full agreementThe terms are written down and signed by both parties and the mediator, and sent to the referring court. The court records them and passes orders or a decree in their terms, for example on a mutual consent divorce petition.
Partial agreementThe agreed points are recorded and sent to the court, and the case continues only on the points that remain in dispute.
No agreementThe mediator reports to the court that mediation did not succeed, without disclosing what was said. The case continues from where it was.

See Matrimonial Settlement Agreements for how agreed terms are written and what they should cover.

Document checklist

  • The court order referring the matter to mediation and the date of the first session
  • Income documents and a monthly budget
  • The children’s school and medical details
  • An inventory of stridhan and belongings
  • A list of every pending case between the parties
