Stridhan & Return of Jewellery: Documenting and Recovering Belongings

Stridhan & Return of Jewellery: Documenting and Recovering Belongings

What stridhan means

Stridhan (literally “a woman’s property”) is property given to a woman, before, at or after her marriage, by her parents, her in-laws, relatives or friends — jewellery, cash, clothes, household items and other gifts. It belongs to her absolutely, and remains hers even if it is kept in the custody of her husband or in-laws. Under the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, property held by a female Hindu is her absolute property.

Holding a woman’s stridhan and refusing to return it on demand can amount to the offence of criminal breach of trust (section 316 BNS, formerly section 406 IPC). The Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 separately requires that any dowry received by anyone other than the woman be transferred to her.

Ways to recover stridhan

  • A written demand, listing the items and asking for their return by a date. Keep proof of delivery.
  • In matrimonial proceedings: section 27 of the Hindu Marriage Act allows the court to make orders about property presented at or about the time of the marriage that belongs jointly to the spouses.
  • Under the domestic violence law: section 19(8) of the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 allows the Magistrate to direct the return of stridhan or other property to which she is entitled.
  • A criminal complaint for criminal breach of trust, where the items were entrusted and are dishonestly withheld.
  • A civil suit for recovery of the items or their value.
  • A settlement, in which the items are listed and returned on a fixed date in a recorded handover.

Sample inventory

The single most useful step is a clear list. Courts and mediators work far more easily with an itemised inventory than with a general claim for “all my jewellery”. A sample format:

Sample stridhan inventory
ItemGiven byCurrently held bySupporting records
Gold necklace set, about 40 gParents, at the weddingMother-in-law (kept in her locker)Wedding photographs showing the set; jeweller’s bill
Two pairs of gold banglesMaternal uncleHusbandPhotograph from the reception; uncle can confirm
Cash, ₹1,00,000Father, by bank transferHusband’s accountBank statement showing the transfer
Refrigerator and washing machineParentsMatrimonial homePurchase invoices; delivery address
Silk sarees (12)In-laws and relativesMatrimonial homePhotographs; list made at the time of the wedding

Add a column for the approximate value if you know it, and keep the list factual — describe each item as precisely as you can (weight, design, colour), so that it can be identified when returned.

When photographs exist but receipts do not

This is the most common situation, because jewellery is often old, inherited, or bought years before the wedding. Lack of a receipt does not mean the claim fails; it means the proof comes from elsewhere. Useful evidence includes:

  • Wedding photographs and videos in which the jewellery is visible. Identify the photograph, the event and the item for each entry in the inventory.
  • Lists made at the time, such as a gift register or a list of items sent with the bride.
  • Testimony of the people who gave the items or who saw them given.
  • Messages that refer to the items, for example a request to keep the jewellery in a locker, or an acknowledgement that it is with the in-laws.
  • A jeweller’s description or valuation made from the photographs.
  • Bank records of money withdrawn or transferred around the time of the wedding.

Document checklist

  • The itemised inventory described above
  • Wedding photographs and videos, with the relevant frames identified
  • Bills, invoices and bank records, where available
  • Gift lists and any written acknowledgement of who holds the items
  • Messages asking for the items and the replies
  • Names of people who gave or saw the items
