Supreme Court on Mutation: Ownership and Dakhil Kharij Explained

A recent Supreme Court judgment, the difference between revenue entries and ownership, and a practical guide for property owners in Lucknow and Uttar Pradesh.

By H.R. Legal Associate · Published 17 October 2026 · 11 minute read

On this page

  1. The judgment
  2. Meaning of mutation
  3. Why mutation still matters
  4. Checks for buyers
  5. Family property
  6. Unexpected mutation
  7. Document checklist
  8. Common questions
  9. Sources

A name appearing in a khatauni or mutation order can look reassuring. For a buyer, it may appear to confirm that the seller owns the land. For a family member, removal of a name may look like the loss of an inherited share. Both situations require closer examination. A revenue entry and the legal right behind it are different matters, and a careful property examination must address both.

The Supreme Court's decision in Jamnabai and Others v. Vasudev and Others, 2026 INSC 900, decided on 20 August 2026, provides a recent illustration. This article explains the decision briefly, then offers practical guidance for buyers, sellers and families dealing with mutation, commonly called dakhil kharij in Uttar Pradesh.

The Supreme Court decision: what happened and what was decided?

The dispute concerned inherited agricultural property in Indore. Ramprasad's name had been removed from the revenue record, and the opposing parties relied on alleged consent and relinquishment documents. His successors sought recognition of their share and consequential relief.

The Supreme Court restored the decrees in their favour. It held that mutation alone could not eliminate an existing ownership interest; the alleged surrender required independent proof. It also rejected treating the date of the revenue entry as an automatic starting point for limitation, and explained the restricted scope of a second appeal. Actual partition remained subject to the applicable Madhya Pradesh law.

Reading reference: paragraphs 19–22 address relinquishment and revenue entries; paragraphs 24–27 discuss limitation and relief; paragraphs 30–32 record the outcome. Read the Supreme Court's official judgment (PDF).

What does mutation actually mean?

Mutation is the process of updating the relevant official record after a reported change in rights. The event behind an application may be a purchase, inheritance, gift, partition or another legally recognised transaction. The application asks the authority to reflect that event in its records. It is therefore important to identify both the record being changed and the legal basis relied upon.

In ordinary conversation, people use “property papers” to describe very different documents. A sale deed, an old khatauni, a municipal assessment, an allotment letter and a tax receipt perform different functions. Keeping them in one file does not make them interchangeable. A useful first step is to label every document by its date, issuing authority and purpose.

For Uttar Pradesh land governed by the Revenue Code, sections 33–35 address reporting succession or transfer and the mutation process. Section 39 preserves the ability to establish rights through the appropriate declaratory proceeding despite specified revenue orders. The applicable route depends on the land and the dispute. These provisions should not be confused with procedures for municipal assessments or development-authority records. [2]

Why mutation is still important for property owners

The distinction between mutation and ownership is a reason to maintain a complete record, not a reason to neglect it. Outdated records can leave an owner repeatedly explaining why a former owner's name appears. An accurate record makes the property file easier to understand and helps identify inconsistencies before a sale, inheritance dispute or financing request makes them urgent.

Mutation papers also preserve a useful chronology. An application, notice, objection, order and resulting entry show what was asserted and what the authority decided. Keep these documents together rather than retaining only a screenshot of the final online entry. If questions arise later, the underlying file may explain matters that a current extract cannot.

After completion, compare the result with the application. Check the person's name, parentage, plot or gata number, area and share. A successful order is of limited practical comfort if the updated extract describes the wrong parcel or records an incorrect extent. Where an error appears, preserve a copy and obtain advice on the appropriate correction procedure.

What buyers should check before paying an advance

A buyer should begin with a simple question: how did this seller acquire the interest being offered? Ask for the document or succession history supporting that answer. Then trace the earlier links far enough to understand the transaction. A seller who inherited property requires a different examination from a seller relying on an allotment, a purchase or a power of attorney.

Build a comparison sheet before agreeing the final documentation. Put the property description in the proposed sale beside the description in earlier deeds and available records. Compare identifying numbers, area, boundaries and the seller's share. Record discrepancies in writing and ask for supporting explanations. An oral assurance that “the numbers changed” should lead to a search for the document recording that change.

Physical identification matters as well. Ask how the parcel shown at the site corresponds to the documents. Clarify access, occupation and whether the proposed sale concerns a demarcated plot or an undivided share. These questions should be settled before substantial payment, when unresolved points can still influence the decision to proceed.

For a property in Lucknow, keep title examination separate from checks concerning land use, layout or building approval and any relevant authority records. A reassuring answer to one question does not answer all the others. Request a written scope of verification so that you know which records were searched, which were unavailable and which issues remain outside the exercise.

Family property: start with the ownership history

When property has passed through several deaths, begin with a family timeline. Record who owned the property, when that person died, who survived at that time and whether any later transfer, will, settlement or court order is asserted. Avoid calculating present shares from a list of relatives who happen to be alive today without examining the earlier stages.

Ask family members to collect the records they already hold before taking an adversarial step. Older deeds, death certificates, correspondence, earlier applications and certified orders may clarify whether the disagreement concerns identity, succession, a transaction or the accuracy of an entry. An organised document exchange can narrow the issue even where agreement on the final share is not yet possible.

Where one relative's name appears alone, investigate the basis of the entry. Obtain the order and the documents submitted with it. Do not assume that the entry proves a valid surrender by everyone else; equally, do not assume wrongdoing merely because one name appears. The papers may disclose an explanation, an omission, a disputed assertion or a transaction requiring further examination.

Inheritance rules and the forum for resolving a dispute depend on the property and the applicable law. A judgment arising under Madhya Pradesh revenue legislation should not be used as a ready-made procedural form for Uttar Pradesh. Before filing, identify the actual relief needed and the authority competent to grant it.

Consent letters, affidavits and relinquishment documents

A request to sign a “simple NOC” deserves careful reading. Ask what the document actually states: does it merely acknowledge receipt of a notice, consent to an administrative correction, record a previous arrangement, or purport to give up a property interest? The heading alone cannot answer that question. Keep an unsigned copy and obtain advice before signing wording that is unclear.

Sections 17 and 49 of the Registration Act, 1908 are relevant when examining instruments that require registration and the consequences of non-registration, subject to their terms and exceptions. Whether a particular family document requires registration depends on its substance and legal effect. Notarisation and registration should not be treated as interchangeable steps. [3]

For practical purposes, preserve the full document, including schedules, endorsements and annexures. A photograph of only the signature page is insufficient for understanding what was agreed. Where a document is disputed, identify the original, who holds it, when it was allegedly signed and the surrounding correspondence. Those details help an advocate assess what evidence should be obtained.

What to do if you discover an unexpected mutation

First, preserve what you found. Save the available extract with its access date and obtain certified copies where appropriate. Write down when and how the change came to your attention. Keep the notice, message, proposed sale advertisement or other material that prompted the discovery. A clear timeline is more useful than a later general statement that the entry was unknown.

Next, seek the underlying mutation order, application, notices, service material and supporting documents. Reading only the current entry can hide the real issue. The application may rely on a deed, succession claim, will, consent letter or earlier order. Identifying that foundation allows the legal response to address the asserted source of rights.

Tell your advocate whether anyone is threatening a sale, construction, dispossession or creation of third-party interests. Supply evidence of that urgency. Different objectives may require different relief: correction of a record, an appeal against an order, a declaration, partition, possession or interim protection. A short factual note and indexed documents help distinguish those objectives.

Do not wait for an informal discussion to finish before having deadlines assessed. A representation or legal notice does not, merely by being sent, stop limitation. Keep proof of every submission and acknowledgement, but obtain advice about the proceeding that actually protects the right in question. The fact that a dispute concerns mutation does not make every possible remedy timeless.

Can mutation be sought on the basis of a will?

This question needs a careful answer. In Tarachandra v. Bhawarlal & Another, 2025 INSC 1485, decided on 19 December 2025, the Supreme Court examined mutation based on a will under Madhya Pradesh legislation. It rejected a blanket exclusion of that mode of acquisition under the provisions considered, while maintaining the distinction between revenue proceedings and final determination of rights. [4]

For someone handling an estate, the practical task is to assemble the will and the relevant ownership and death records, identify the people affected and disclose any challenge already raised. Registration of a will should not be treated as a substitute for examining its execution or a genuine dispute about it. Nor should a rule drawn from one state's mutation legislation be applied elsewhere without checking the governing provisions.

A practical document checklist

The precise requirements vary, but the following starting file can make a consultation more productive. Mark missing items clearly rather than replacing them with assumptions:

  • Property identity: address, village, tehsil, district, gata or survey number, area and boundaries available in the papers.
  • Ownership documents: current and earlier deeds, allotment papers, relevant orders and any asserted settlement or release.
  • Revenue history: current and older extracts, mutation orders, applications, objections and proof of service available to you.
  • Succession material: death certificates, a family timeline, any will and documents supporting the asserted relationship.
  • Possession information: who occupies or uses the property, since when, and the documents or correspondence explaining that occupation.
  • Dispute chronology: dated demands, replies, notices, advertisements, pending proceedings and the first discovery of the disputed entry.
  • Transaction concerns: the proposed agreement, payment requests, access details and unresolved discrepancies requiring a written answer.

Use a numbered index and retain originals securely. Give copies for initial review and note which documents are certified, downloaded or supplied by another person. This makes the limitations of the file visible from the beginning and helps avoid an opinion being misunderstood as covering records that were never examined.

Three examples of how to approach the papers

A buyer receives only a khatauni: ask for the seller's acquisition documents and explain that the file is incomplete for purchase verification. Obtain the earlier links, compare the parcel and identify outstanding searches before deciding whether to commit funds. The immediate problem is missing evidence, and the useful next step is a defined document requisition.

A sibling discovers a changed entry after a parent's death: gather the death and ownership records, list the family circumstances at the relevant dates and obtain the mutation file. Identify whether a document allegedly signed by the parent or another heir is being relied upon. Seek advice on both the entry and the underlying claim before choosing a remedy.

A seller has a deed but outdated records: prepare an explanation of the sequence, locate earlier applications and check the available route for updating the record. Disclose the position to the prospective buyer. Trying to hide an administrative mismatch can create avoidable distrust even where the underlying documents may support the seller's case.

Frequently asked questions

Should I ignore mutation because it is not a title document?

No. Maintain accurate records and retain the order as part of the property file. The useful approach is to examine the legal basis and the administrative record together, identifying what each document establishes and what further verification is needed.

Is a registered document the end of verification?

It should be read as part of the complete transaction history. Ask about the executant's authority, the interest conveyed, property identity and any restrictions or competing claims requiring examination. Define the scope of searches before treating the file as complete.

Can every mutation dispute be filed in an ordinary civil court?

No single forum can be assumed for every case. Agricultural tenure, the nature of the claim, special statutory provisions and the relief sought can affect jurisdiction. Have the appropriate revenue or civil remedy identified for the actual property.

Should I publish or rely on a headline saying ownership is now cancelled?

Avoid that description. Read the identified judgment and its context. A clear explanation should distinguish the record, the underlying transaction and the remedy sought, rather than suggest that every existing mutation or property transaction has automatically become invalid.
