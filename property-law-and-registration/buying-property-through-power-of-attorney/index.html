Buying Property Through a Power of Attorney: Legal Risks and Essential Checks

A practical guide to genuine attorney-led property sales, the limits of GPA arrangements and the checks to complete before paying for property.

Can you safely buy property through a power of attorney?

Buying property through a power of attorney can be legitimate when an authorised representative signs the necessary conveyance for the actual owner. The danger begins when a buyer treats the power of attorney itself as ownership. Those are different transactions, and confusing them can leave someone paying for a home without acquiring the title they expected.

A power of attorney, often called a POA or GPA, creates authority to act. It does not automatically establish that the principal owns the property, that the authority remains effective or that a proposed sale complies with registration and other applicable laws. Each question needs separate verification.

This guide explains the distinction, the documents to examine and the warning signs worth addressing before paying a deposit. It is particularly relevant to buyers in Lucknow and Uttar Pradesh, while recognising that local registration, stamp and land rules require transaction-specific checks.

What a property power of attorney actually does

The principal is the person granting authority; the attorney is the person authorised to act. In this context, “attorney” does not necessarily mean a lawyer. It may be a relative, employee or another representative. Their authority comes from the instrument and the governing law, not simply from their relationship with the owner.

A general power of attorney may cover several matters. A special power of attorney usually addresses defined acts or a particular transaction. The label is less important than the operative wording. A document called a GPA might contain limited powers, while a carefully drafted special POA might expressly authorise execution of a sale deed.

Authority to manage property, collect rent or pay municipal charges should not be assumed to include authority to sell. Likewise, authority to sign does not automatically resolve whether the attorney can receive consideration, negotiate terms, deliver possession or appoint someone else.

A GPA sale is different from a sale executed by an attorney

In informal property dealings, “GPA sale” can describe a package of an agreement to sell, power of attorney, payment receipt, possession letter and sometimes a Will. Buyers may believe this bundle gives them the same ownership as a registered conveyance. That assumption creates substantial risk.

The Supreme Court’s decision in Suraj Lamp & Industries Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Haryana explains the foundational distinction: agreement/GPA/Will arrangements do not operate as substitutes for a registered conveyance transferring title. An agreement to sell is not itself a completed transfer of ownership.

That principle does not prohibit genuine agency arrangements. An owner may authorise a representative to execute an appropriate sale deed, subject to the relevant legal requirements. The transaction must be assessed as a conveyance by the owner acting through an authorised person, rather than as a purchase of the POA document.

First verify the owner’s title, not just the attorney’s papers

A perfectly genuine POA cannot give the principal ownership they never had. Begin with the chain of title: how the present owner acquired the property, whether the earlier transfers are properly documented and whether the property descriptions remain consistent across the records.

Examine the relevant conveyances, allotment or lease documents, inheritance records, partition arrangements and other material affecting ownership. Revenue and municipal records can assist the investigation, but should be read alongside the title documents. A tax receipt or mutation entry should not be presented as conclusive ownership evidence.

Check for mortgages, prior agreements, litigation, attachments, acquisition proceedings and restrictions associated with the property. A registration search is useful, but it does not necessarily reveal every unregistered claim, possession dispute or statutory restriction. The scope of the search should reflect the property and its history.

Where there are co-owners, identify each person’s interest. An attorney appointed by one owner cannot automatically sell everyone else’s shares. Family assurances are not a replacement for the legal participation or authority of those whose rights are being transferred.

Read the authority clause against the proposed sale

Ask for the complete POA, including schedules, endorsements and annexures. A photograph of the signature page is insufficient. Match the principal’s identity, the attorney’s identity and the property description with the proposed sale deed and original ownership documents.

Look for express authority covering the acts required by the transaction. Depending on the arrangement, these may include agreeing terms, executing the conveyance, appearing before the registration authority, admitting execution, receiving money and delivering possession. Conditions attached to any power matter just as much as the power itself.

Check whether the authority is limited by time, purpose, minimum price, a named buyer or a requirement for prior consent. If the attorney proposes acting through a substitute, examine whether delegation is permitted and whether the substitute’s authority has been validly created.

Be particularly cautious if the attorney is also the buyer, proposes selling to a close associate or controls both sides of the payment. These circumstances require scrutiny of informed consent, express authority and conflicts of interest; a broad management clause is not a comfortable substitute.

Is the POA genuine and still operative?

Meet or independently contact the principal wherever practicable. Use contact details verified independently of the attorney’s messages. Confirm that the principal knows about the proposed property, buyer, price and payment arrangements, and record the confirmation appropriately.

Inspect the original instrument and verify available registration or authentication particulars through the responsible authority. A stamp, seal or online entry should be checked for what it actually proves. Formal authentication does not establish that the principal had good title or that every statement in the document is true.

Investigate possible revocation, expiry, completion of the authorised purpose or other events affecting the agency. Ask whether any cancellation, dispute or competing POA exists. A fresh confirmation is useful evidence, although it does not replace the necessary legal examination.

Death or loss of capacity can affect an agency. Do not accept a claim that an old POA necessarily remains usable after the principal has died. The position must be examined under the agency rules and any relevant exception before execution proceeds.

Why “irrevocable GPA” is not a complete answer

Some documents prominently use the word “irrevocable”. That description does not by itself make the attorney an owner or guarantee that the authority survives every event. The substance of the arrangement and the law governing agency remain important.

Section 202 of the Indian Contract Act addresses an agency in which the agent has an interest in the subject matter. Whether a particular arrangement meets that requirement is a legal question. Simply writing “irrevocable”, or pointing to a payment without examining its context, does not settle it.

Even where an agency is protected against termination in particular circumstances, a buyer must still address the conveyance, title, consideration, stamp duty and registration requirements. The exception should not be used as a shortcut around a properly documented transfer.

Registration, authentication, notarisation and stamping

A notarised POA and a registered sale deed serve different purposes. Notarisation should not be treated as a substitute for the conveyance required to transfer ownership. Nor should registration of a POA be mistaken for registration of a sale.

The Registration Act’s rules about who may present documents and which powers of attorney are recognised require careful application. Sections 32 and 33 are relevant, but the answer can depend on whether the attorney executes the document on the principal’s behalf or merely presents a document executed by someone else.

For that reason, avoid universal statements that every property POA must follow exactly the same formality. Examine the instrument, the act being performed, the principal’s circumstances, applicable state amendments and the requirements of the responsible registration office.

Stamp treatment is another separate question. The nature of the authority, consideration, relationship of the parties and local law may matter. Confirm the applicable duty and any adjudication requirements before signing or presenting documents. An attractive fee estimate from an intermediary is not a legal assessment.

Buying when the owner is abroad or an NRI

An overseas owner can create a genuine need for representation, but distance increases the importance of reliable identity checks. Confirm the principal’s identity, location, ownership and understanding of the transaction. Avoid relying entirely on calls arranged and controlled by the proposed attorney.

The appropriate method of execution or authentication may involve consular procedures, notarisation, an apostille or another recognised route, depending on the circumstances. These are not interchangeable labels. Verify what Indian registration law and the local authority require for the particular document.

Documents executed outside India may also require attention to Indian stamping and related procedural requirements after receipt. Do not assume that an apostille or overseas notarial seal completes every Indian formality. Obtain advice before relying on the instrument for registration.

Where the seller is non-resident, examine foreign-exchange compliance, property-category restrictions and tax withholding separately. Residence and tax status cannot safely be inferred from citizenship alone. Agricultural land, plantation property and similar categories can raise additional issues that an ordinary residential transaction does not.

Also compare the property on the ground with the documents. Boundaries, unit numbers, area, access and possession should be investigated. A correctly authenticated POA cannot resolve a mismatch between the flat you visited and the property identified in the title schedule.

For development-authority allotments, leasehold interests or housing arrangements with transfer conditions, check the governing documents and required permissions. Selling through an attorney does not remove restrictions attached to the property itself.

Structure the agreement, payment and completion carefully

The agreement should identify the owner as the seller acting through the named attorney and explain the authority being relied on. It should record the documents to be supplied, necessary permissions, payment stages, completion obligations and the consequences of a material title or authority defect.

Do not release a substantial deposit merely because the attorney promises to produce the original POA later. Make essential verification a condition before money is put at risk. Where a document is missing, identify whether it can actually be supplied rather than treating every gap as an administrative formality.

Use traceable payment channels and check the payee’s identity. If payment is to be received by the attorney or another person, obtain a clear legal basis and properly documented instructions. Avoid treating a personal receipt as equivalent to the owner’s valid discharge of the purchase price.

At completion, ensure the sale deed accurately reflects the representative capacity and property description. Collect the required originals, registration records and possession documentation. Afterwards, pursue the appropriate record updates while remembering that mutation records the change rather than replacing the conveyance.

Pressure to complete immediately is another warning sign, especially where there is no opportunity for an independent document review. A genuine seller should be able to explain the transaction and provide the records needed for reasonable verification.

What if you already bought through a GPA arrangement?

Do not assume either that every existing arrangement is worthless or that long possession has automatically cured all defects. Assemble the entire file: agreements, POA, receipts, possession records, correspondence, prior title documents and any Will relied on. The available rights and remedies depend on those facts.

Where the true owner can lawfully execute a proper conveyance, completing the necessary transfer may be an option. That requires checking continuing ownership, consent, encumbrances, authority and applicable charges; it is not simply a clerical replacement of one paper with another.

If fraud, competing sales, revocation or a deceased principal is involved, obtain advice before making further payments or signing a new instrument. Potential contractual, civil or criminal remedies require individual assessment, including procedural requirements and applicable time limits. No particular outcome follows from the label “GPA sale”.

Frequently asked questions

Can a registered power of attorney transfer property ownership?

Registration of the POA does not make it a sale deed. Ownership transfer must be examined through the legally required conveyance and the seller’s underlying title.

Can an attorney sign a sale deed for an owner?

A properly authorised attorney can act for the owner, subject to valid authority, applicable formalities and restrictions. The transaction must be examined on its documents.

Does a Will make a GPA purchase safe?

A Will is testamentary and is not a present conveyance to the buyer. Adding it to a document bundle does not turn that bundle into a completed sale.

Is mutation enough after paying through a GPA?

No. Mutation serves administrative or fiscal purposes and does not independently establish the ownership transfer that a missing conveyance would otherwise need to achieve.

Is buying through an NRI’s attorney always risky?

Overseas representation can be legitimate. The safeguards are verified title, genuine and operative authority, correct execution and authentication, and compliance with registration, tax and foreign-exchange requirements.

Legal references and transaction-specific advice

The main legal framework includes the Transfer of Property Act, Registration Act, Indian Contract Act and Powers-of-Attorney Act, together with applicable state amendments and stamp law. Read these alongside the Supreme Court’s Suraj Lamp distinction between informal GPA arrangements and genuine authorised conveyances.

Consult India Code for legislation, the Supreme Court of India for judgments, IGRS Uttar Pradesh for local registration information and the Reserve Bank of India for relevant foreign-exchange directions.
