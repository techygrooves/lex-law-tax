Mortgage Property Dispute Lawyer in Lucknow

What kind of mortgage you have changes everything

The Transfer of Property Act recognises several kinds of mortgage, and the kind decides what the lender can do without going to court. Borrowers frequently do not know which they have signed, and it is the first thing to establish.

The most common in housing and business lending are the simple mortgage, where the mortgagee has no possession and must go to court to realise the security, and the equitable mortgage created by deposit of title deeds, which is how a great deal of institutional lending is secured and is why the bank holds your originals. An English mortgage transfers the property absolutely subject to a proviso for retransfer on repayment. A mortgage by conditional sale is drafted as a sale that becomes absolute on default, and is the one most often disputed, because the question whether a document is a mortgage or an outright sale with a buy-back arrangement is litigated constantly.

Running alongside this is a separate and much more powerful regime. Where the lender is a bank or financial institution covered by the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002 — SARFAESI — it can enforce the security without a court at all. That is the position most borrowers in default are actually in, and it is a different world from a mortgage suit.

The SARFAESI timetable

SARFAESI enforcement runs on a defined sequence, and each stage carries a remedy that is lost if it is not taken.

It begins once the account is classified as non-performing, with a demand notice under Section 13(2) calling on the borrower to discharge the liability within sixty days and stating the amount claimed. That notice is the foundation of everything that follows, and its contents matter later — the amount stated in it is generally what determines the pre-deposit on any eventual appeal.

The borrower may make a representation or objection, and the secured creditor is obliged to consider it and to communicate reasons if it is not accepted. This stage is skipped by most borrowers and it should not be. A reasoned objection on the record — disputing the classification, the computation, or the existence of default — is both a real opportunity and the foundation of a later challenge.

If the demand is not met, the lender may take measures under Section 13(4): take possession of the secured asset, take over management, or sell it. Possession is commonly taken first symbolically and then physically, with the assistance of the District Magistrate where required.

The borrower's remedy against those measures is an application to the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17, to be made within the period the Act allows. The scope of that enquiry is limited to the steps taken under Section 13(4) against the secured assets — it is not a general audit of the banking relationship, and applications framed as though it were tend to fail.

The pre-deposit that shapes every appeal

An appeal from the Tribunal to the Appellate Tribunal under Section 18 carries a condition that borrowers are rarely prepared for: a pre-deposit of fifty per cent of the amount of debt due, as claimed by the secured creditor or as determined by the Tribunal, whichever is less. The Appellate Tribunal may reduce it, but not below twenty-five per cent. Courts treat it as a condition precedent to the maintainability of the appeal.

The Supreme Court has explained what the 'debt due' means for this purpose. Where the challenge is to steps under Sections 13(2) and 13(4), the amount in the Section 13(2) notice generally governs. Where the auction sale is under challenge, the amount of the sale certificate governs. Where both are challenged, the higher figure applies. The Court also held that a borrower cannot claim adjustment of the sale proceeds against the deposit where the auction itself is what is being challenged.

Two practical consequences follow. First, the appeal has to be planned financially from the moment the Tribunal's order is anticipated, not when it arrives. Second, the pre-deposit is not a payment to the lender: it has been held not to be a secured asset, and it is ordinarily refundable once the appeal is disposed of, subject to exceptions.

There is also a developing line on whether appeals against purely procedural orders of the Tribunal attract the requirement at all. The Supreme Court has taken a prima facie view that they may not, and the question has been examined by High Courts since. Where the order under appeal is procedural rather than final, that argument is worth considering, but it is an evolving area and the current position should be checked.

Redemption: the right that outlives most defences

The borrower's strongest and most underused right is the right of redemption — to pay what is due and get the property back. The principle behind it is old and firmly held: once a mortgage, always a mortgage, and a mortgagee cannot by drafting convert a security into an outright acquisition.

The critical question in practice is timing — until when can the property be redeemed. In a SARFAESI enforcement the answer is bound up with the stage the sale has reached, and the position has been the subject of significant litigation, with the law as amended fixing the point by reference to the publication of the sale notice rather than the completion of the sale. For a borrower trying to save a property, that makes the difference between acting now and acting too late.

The practical lesson is blunt. A borrower who can raise the money — by refinancing, by selling the property themselves at a better price than an auction will fetch, or from family — should do it as early in the process as possible. Every stage that passes narrows the options, and an auction sale once completed is far harder to unwind than it is to pre-empt.

Challenging an auction

Auction sales under SARFAESI are challenged frequently and successfully less often than borrowers expect, because the grounds that work are procedural rather than about price alone.

What a borrower can properly attack is a failure to follow the prescribed procedure: inadequate notice of sale, non-compliance with the publication requirements, a reserve price fixed without proper valuation, a sale conducted in a manner that suppressed competition, or a material irregularity in the conduct of the auction. Where the property has sold at a gross undervalue, that is evidence that something went wrong in the process rather than a ground on its own.

What generally does not work is an objection that the borrower could have arranged better terms, or that the lender was unreasonable in classifying the account, raised for the first time after the sale. The time for those arguments is at the Section 13(2) and Section 17 stages.

The position of the auction purchaser also hardens as the process completes. A challenge brought promptly, before the sale certificate issues and possession passes, is in a materially different position from one brought afterwards.

Private lenders and disguised mortgages

Away from institutional lending, a different pattern recurs in Lucknow and across Uttar Pradesh: money is lent privately against property, and the security is documented not as a mortgage but as a sale deed with an unregistered agreement to re-convey on repayment.

These arrangements are the source of a great deal of litigation, and the question is always the same — was the transaction a mortgage, or was it a sale? The law looks at substance. The relative value of the property and the loan, who remained in possession, whether interest was being paid, how the parties treated the property afterwards, and whether the relationship of debtor and creditor continued are all examined.

A borrower in this position is in a much better place than they usually assume, because if the transaction is held to be a mortgage, the right of redemption survives and the lender cannot keep the property simply because repayment was late. But establishing it requires evidence of the surrounding circumstances, and that evidence degrades with time. Anyone in such an arrangement should take advice while the facts are still provable rather than when the lender finally asserts ownership.

Guarantors, co-borrowers and the family home

Enforcement rarely affects only the borrower, and the people around the borrower are often unaware of their exposure until a notice arrives.

A guarantor’s liability under Indian law is ordinarily co-extensive with the principal debtor’s, and it does not depend on the lender exhausting its remedies against the borrower first. A guarantor can be proceeded against directly, and property given as security by a guarantor can be enforced against even though the guarantor received none of the money. Family members who signed as guarantors years ago, as a formality for a relative’s business loan, are regularly surprised by this.

Co-borrowers are in a different position again: they are borrowers, not sureties, and arguments available to a guarantor are not available to them. Where a spouse or parent was added as a co-borrower to improve eligibility, they carry full liability.

The family home raises its own issues. Where the property secured is the residence of people who are not the borrower — a parent with a right of residence, a family with an interest in ancestral property — the extent to which the security binds their interest depends on who held what and who joined in the security. A mortgage created by one co-sharer does not ordinarily bind the others’ shares, and that is sometimes the strongest point available.

The practical advice is unwelcome but consistent: anyone asked to sign as guarantor or co-borrower for property-secured lending should understand that they are being asked to put that property at risk, and should take the request as seriously as the borrower does.

How the firm handles mortgage disputes

The first step is to establish exactly where in the sequence the matter stands, because the available remedies are stage-dependent and several of them expire. The documents needed are the loan and security documents, every notice received with its date of receipt, the account statement, and any correspondence with the lender.

From there the questions are whether there is a substantive answer on the merits, what the realistic prospect of redemption is, and — where an appeal is in contemplation — how the pre-deposit will be funded. Those are commercial as well as legal judgments and they are better made early.

The firm acts for borrowers in applications before the Debts Recovery Tribunal and in appeals, in challenges to auction sales, and in civil proceedings concerning private mortgages, redemption and transactions documented as sales. No outcome is promised in any matter.
