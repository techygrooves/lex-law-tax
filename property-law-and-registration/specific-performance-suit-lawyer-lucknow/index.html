A specific performance suit seeks completion of the property contract

A specific performance suit lawyer in Lucknow helps enforce a binding agreement where the promised property transaction has not been completed. A buyer might have paid an advance, arranged funds and requested registration while the seller refuses to sign, demands a higher price or proposes selling elsewhere. The legal question is whether the contract should be enforced through a court decree.

Specific performance is different from a simple refund claim. The principal objective is performance of the contractual obligation, such as execution of a sale deed. Compensation, refund, possession and other connected reliefs must be considered separately and pleaded where appropriate.

The agreement is the starting point, but the case is built from the complete transaction: the parties’ obligations, property identity, payment arrangement, communications, readiness to complete and the reason performance failed.

The statutory framework after the 2018 amendment

The Specific Relief Act governs the remedy. The 2018 amendment changed the framework, including Section 10, and specific performance must be examined alongside the statutory exclusions and conditions in provisions such as Sections 14 and 16.

The remedy is not automatic simply because a signed agreement exists. The contract must be legally enforceable, the claimant must satisfy the applicable requirements and the transaction must be capable of the performance sought.

The applicable version of the law and the transaction’s dates also matter. The amendment should not be treated as an answer that eliminates every issue in older contracts or proceedings. Identify the agreement, cause of action and legal framework before drafting the relief.

A strong suit connects each statutory requirement to evidence. It explains what was promised, what the claimant was required to do, what steps were taken and how the defendant failed to perform.

What makes a property agreement enforceable?

The parties, property and essential terms must be sufficiently certain. A document that leaves the actual plot, price or obligation unresolved creates problems different from a complete agreement followed by a refusal to register.

Identify who contracted and their authority. A company representative, co-owner or attorney can bind only the interests and obligations the law and authority permit. An agreement signed by one co-owner does not automatically promise every other owner’s share.

Examine conditions concerning title documents, permissions, payment milestones, vacant possession and the execution date. Some obligations are reciprocal: the claimant’s performance may be linked to the seller supplying documents or doing something agreed first.

Registration and stamping require separate attention. The legal use of an agreement in evidence, the effect of insufficient stamping and the distinction between an agreement and a completed conveyance should be addressed directly. Paying an advance does not itself transfer ownership.

Readiness and willingness: the central evidence

Section 16 contains important personal bars to relief, including the requirement relating to performance and continuing readiness and willingness. The claimant’s case should show compliance with essential obligations, subject to the statutory framework and the other party’s conduct.

Readiness includes the practical ability to perform, including the financial arrangements required by the agreement. Willingness is demonstrated through conduct. Bank records without any genuine attempt to complete, or repeated demands unsupported by available means, can leave important gaps.

Useful evidence includes funds, sanctioned finance where relevant, payment records, written requests for documents, proposed registration arrangements and responses to the seller’s objections. The claimant should explain how the balance would have been paid under the agreed transaction.

The law does not reduce readiness to one mandatory ritual in every case. Actual tender or deposit has to be understood through the applicable provision and any court direction. What matters is a consistent, evidenced account of the claimant’s obligations and ability to perform.

Notices and a clear completion record

A well-drafted notice identifies the agreement, property, payments, outstanding obligations and the performance requested. It should state a coherent position rather than alternate casually between abandoning the transaction and insisting that it must be completed.

Keep proof of dispatch, delivery and the full reply. Communications can establish refusal, requests for more time, admissions about payment or the reason registration did not occur. Preserve original electronic messages and records needed to establish authenticity.

Where the seller raises a title defect or missing permission, address it through the agreement’s allocation of obligations. An unreasonable demand to complete without required documents can be as problematic as a claimant who never responds to a genuine completion proposal.

Attendance material from a proposed registration appointment can be useful, but it should be connected to the transaction. A photograph outside an office does not by itself establish every element of readiness and willingness.

Article 54: three years, with two different starting points

Article 54 of the Limitation Act provides three years for a suit for specific performance. The starting point is the date fixed for performance or, where no such date is fixed, when the claimant has notice that performance is refused.

This is why “three years from signing the agreement” is an unreliable shortcut. The agreement, its conditions, any extension and the communicated refusal must be examined. A fixed date and an open-ended arrangement do not necessarily begin the period in the same way.

A later notice does not automatically restart an already running limitation period. Nor should every negotiation be treated as a legally effective extension or acknowledgment without examining its terms and the applicable law.

Create a dated chronology at the beginning: agreement, payment, conditions fulfilled, completion date, extensions, requests, refusal and previous proceedings. The limitation analysis should be visible in the drafting rather than left to an assumption.

When the seller transfers to someone else

A subsequent transfer can complicate the transaction but does not make the earlier agreement irrelevant. Section 19 addresses enforcement against specified persons, including subsequent transferees within its framework and the protection available to a qualifying transferee for value in good faith without notice.

The later purchaser’s knowledge, consideration and circumstances can become important evidence. The agreement-holder should obtain the later instrument, identify the purchaser and assess the relief and parties required.

Lis pendens under Section 52 of the Transfer of Property Act addresses dealings during qualifying litigation. It does not mean every later deed is automatically void. A specific injunction against further dealing should be considered on its own legal basis.

Refund, compensation and substituted performance

A claimant should examine alternative or additional remedies before filing. Depending on the case, the prayers can address refund of amounts paid, compensation, possession or other legally available relief. Pleading those alternatives properly prevents an unnecessarily incomplete case.

Section 20 provides a framework for substituted performance, including a written notice requirement of at least thirty days in the circumstances governed by it. Obtaining substituted performance has important consequences for a later claim to specific performance of the same contract.

This option should therefore be selected deliberately. A buyer should not assume that arranging an alternative transaction, issuing an informal message and continuing to insist on the original conveyance all have the same legal effect.

A contractual refund or liquidated-damages clause also requires interpretation. Its presence does not automatically answer whether specific performance is excluded. Read the complete agreement and the governing provisions before treating one clause as decisive.

How the suit moves from filing to evidence

The plaint should identify the agreement, parties, property, obligations, breach, limitation position and reliefs. It should include the necessary parties and explain the valuation and jurisdictional basis.

The defendant’s written statement can raise issues such as lack of authority, uncertain terms, alleged default, absence of readiness, disputed payment or a later valid transfer. The claimant’s evidence should be organised around those actual issues.

Documents and testimony are tested at trial. The original agreement, receipts, bank records and complete correspondence are more useful than a selective bundle of favourable messages. Where a signature or execution is denied, the required proof must be prepared.

For Lucknow property, territorial jurisdiction generally follows the rules governing the property and the relief sought. Pecuniary competence, land classification and special statutory restrictions require attention as well. A contract concerning restricted or specially regulated land cannot be assessed as an ordinary urban resale without further analysis.

What happens after a decree for specific performance?

The decree sets out the obligations to be completed, including payment, execution and any other directions. Compliance with those terms is essential. A claimant should keep proof of every payment or deposit made under the court’s directions.

If the judgment-debtor does not execute the required document, the Code of Civil Procedure provides an execution mechanism, including the procedure associated with Order XXI Rule 34. The draft instrument and objections are dealt with through the court’s process.

Possession requires its own attention in the prayers and decree. A buyer should not assume that an order concerning execution of a sale deed automatically answers every occupation or handover issue.

After the conveyance is completed, preserve the executed instrument and pursue the appropriate registration and record updates. The final practical outcome should match the contractual property and the court’s directions.

Example: the seller demands more after the buyer arranges funds

A buyer agrees to purchase a Lucknow house, pays the recorded advance and arranges the balance within the agreed framework. Before registration, the seller demands a substantially higher price because market values have risen.

The case should establish the binding terms, payments, ability to complete, communications and the seller’s refusal. A demand for more money becomes part of the evidence; it does not remove the need to prove the claimant’s own position.

If the seller also proposes another sale, interim protection and the role of any later purchaser require attention. The relief should be drafted to complete the transaction while addressing the connected risks, rather than simply describing the seller’s conduct as unfair.

Build a performance file, not just a payment file

A collection of advance receipts proves only part of a specific performance case. The performance file should explain every essential step: title documents requested, permissions required, finance arranged, the proposed mode of payment and communications fixing or discussing completion.

Readiness must match the actual contract. A buyer offering payment in a mode the agreement does not permit, or demanding an entirely different property, needs to address that inconsistency. The court is asked to enforce the bargain proved, not write a new one that the claimant now prefers.

Where the seller had to clear an encumbrance or supply a document first, identify the obligation and the evidence showing its status. Reciprocal obligations should be presented in sequence. This prevents a seller’s unperformed duty from disappearing behind a general allegation that the buyer failed to attend registration.

Financing evidence should be realistic. Identify the available funds, sanctioned facility or other arrangement relied on and the conditions attached to it. A proposal dependent on events that never occurred needs an explanation, particularly when the agreement required completion through a defined payment arrangement.

Settlement can still be explored while preserving a coherent legal position. Record whether an offer is a proposal to complete, an alternative refund arrangement or a compromise of the entire dispute. Avoid signing a full discharge while assuming the original conveyance claim remains unchanged.

Where several buyers or sellers are involved, identify the obligation of each. Authority, ownership shares and the agreed performance can differ. A decree must be capable of operating against the persons bound by the transaction and in favour of those entitled to enforce it.

The final relief should also address practical completion. Identify the form of the conveyance, the interest to be transferred, payment obligations and possession. A successful suit is most useful when its decree can be implemented through a clear document and a recognisable handover, rather than leaving essential terms for another dispute.

A buyer should also preserve evidence of responding to genuine completion proposals. If the seller suggests a date, sends draft papers or requests an agreed document, record the response and the reason for any objection. Consistency strengthens the account of willingness. The same applies to an extension: preserve the complete exchange showing its terms and acceptance, rather than relying on one isolated message. A clear record distinguishes a genuine effort to complete from negotiations that never produced a settled variation.

Frequently asked questions

Can I demand the property instead of accepting a refund?

An enforceable agreement can support a claim for specific performance subject to the statutory requirements and facts. The contract and the claimant’s performance record are central.

Does paying an advance make me the owner?

No. An agreement and advance do not by themselves replace the conveyance required to transfer ownership.

Is a legal notice enough to prove readiness?

A notice is part of the evidence. Financial ability, conduct and compliance with contractual obligations must also be established.

What if the agreement has no completion date?

Article 54 addresses the situation where no date is fixed by referring to notice of refusal. The communications and chronology become particularly important.

Can the seller’s later purchaser be included?

The later transaction and Section 19 framework determine the parties and reliefs needed. Obtain the subsequent deed and examine notice and good faith.
